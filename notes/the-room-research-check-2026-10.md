# The Room: research check (October 2026)

Prepared 2026-10-20 as a companion to `notes/the-room-revision-brief-2026-10.md`.
Checks every research claim in `ao-knowledge-hq-kit/knowledge/the-room/the-room.md`
(line numbers refer to that file). Use this when drafting the revision.

Verdicts: **OK** (matches the source), **WRONG** (the source says something
different), **UNSOURCED** (no source found or named), **LOOSE** (real source,
but the book stretches it).

## Chapter 5 and 6: the coaching study (most important)

**Line 354. WRONG.** Coutu and Kauffman, "What Can Coaches Do for You?", HBR,
January 2009. It is a survey of 140 executive coaches about why companies hire
them. It did not study leaders or measure which leaders benefited most. No
summary of it contains "thinking partner with no stake in the outcome"; treat
that phrase as the book's own wording, not a quote.

What the study does support (as reported in summaries of the full HBR research
report, "The Realities of Executive Coaching"):
- Top reasons coaches were hired: 48% develop high-potential people or support a
  transition, 26% act as a sounding board, 12% address a derailing behavior.
- Coaches said that ten years earlier most were hired to fix toxic behavior at
  the top; now most work is developing capable, high-performing people.

That supports "not just for crisis" honestly. It does not support "the leaders
who benefited most were not the ones in crisis."

**Line 455. UNSOURCED.** "The leaders who report the highest value ... are
consistently the ones who engaged during periods of strength." No source. Cut
it or tie it to the coaching numbers above.

**Bibliography, line 525. OK (resolved 2026-10-20).** Pages 91 to 97 is correct.
The piece is HBR's research report by Coutu and Kauffman plus commentary from
five experts (Ram Charan, David B. Peterson, Michael Maccoby, P. Anne Scoular,
Anthony M. Grant). Academic reference lists cite it as 87(1), 91-97. The
EconBiz catalog's 91 to 93 covers only the report section.

## Chapter 3: costs

**Line 194, Department of Labor 30%. UNSOURCED.** Widely repeated online, but no
one links to an actual Department of Labor publication. The bibliography entry
"U.S. Department of Labor (2012), The Cost of a Bad Hire" does not appear to
exist.

**Line 194, SHRM 50 to 60%. UNSOURCED.** Blogs attribute many different numbers
to SHRM (about 40%, up to five times salary). None link to a SHRM report. The
bibliography entry "SHRM Foundation (2016), The True Cost of a Bad Hire" was not
found.

**Line 194, "HBR and Gallup, 1.5 to 4 times." UNSOURCED** as written. No specific
article named.

**Line 200, McKinsey 530 hours. WRONG.** McKinsey, "Three keys to faster, better
decisions" (2019), survey of about 1,200 managers: about 530,000 **days** of
manager time potentially wasted per year at a typical Fortune 500 company, about
$250 million in wages. The book's add-on ("traceable to decisions that required
revisiting because critical input was absent") is not in the source. McKinsey is
not in the bibliography.

**Line 200, Bain 28%. WRONG source.** The 28% comes from McKinsey, "The case for
behavioral strategy" (March 2010), a survey of 2,207 executives: 28% said their
company's strategic decisions were generally good, 60% said bad decisions were
about as common as good ones, 12% said good ones were rare. The second half of
the sentence ("despite ... believed their teams communicated openly") is not in
the source.

**Line 186. UNSOURCED.** "A study examining post-mortem analyses of major
organizational decisions" is not named and has no bibliography entry.

## Chapters 2 and 4

**Lines 133 and 157, Detert and Edmondson (2011). LOOSE.** The paper is real and
correctly cited. It found that people hold unspoken rules about when speaking up
is risky, and those rules predict silence beyond the usual explanations. But the
rules are about protecting yourself from risk, so "not out of fear" overstates
it. "What their voice will actually change" (futility) is a different line of
research. Suggested framing: unspoken, self-protective rules about when it is
safe to speak, which keep capable people quiet even in workplaces that feel
healthy.

**Line 133, "information asymmetry in hierarchical systems." UNSOURCED.** Vague
appeal to "studies." Cut or attribute.

**Line 214, Klein and Weick. LOOSE but acceptable.** Weick (sensemaking) fits
well. Klein's work is about how experienced people decide quickly by recognizing
patterns; the "coherent narrative" point is a stretch for Klein.

**Line 259, Gallup and Towers Watson on survey fatigue. UNSOURCED** as written.
Those are engagement reports. Neither is known for showing employees adjust
survey answers based on fear of consequence.

**Line 283, Maccoby (2000). OK.** Real article, correctly cited; the book on
productive narcissists exists (2003). The "surround themselves with people who
reinforce" summary is a fair reading.

**Line 350, Roman emperor and medieval king. LOOSE.** Roman emperors' councils
included senators. Suggest softening to "rulers have long kept counselors who
stood outside the usual ladder of power."

## Bibliography entries that check out
Blenko, Mankins and Rogers (2010); Detert and Edmondson (2011); Edmondson (1999);
Gallup (2017); Klein (1998); Maccoby (2000); Towers Watson (2014); Weick (1995).
Note: Blenko et al. is cited in the bibliography but the text never uses it
(the "Bain" claim actually comes from McKinsey). Its real finding (decision
effectiveness tracks closely with financial results) could replace the 28% line.
