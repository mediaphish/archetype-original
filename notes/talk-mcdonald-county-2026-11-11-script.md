**SCREEN: The elephant in the room**
- Some jobs will change. Some will go away.
- Repetitive, button-pushing work goes first.
- Even work we called safe a year ago.
- RemAIning Human is free with code MCCFREE.
- The QR code to the RemAIning Human page

Now I owe you the honest part. I just told you not to panic. That's not the same as telling you nothing is changing.

Some jobs are at risk. If most of a job is pushing the same buttons, moving information from one screen to another, or answering the same questions the same way, AI is going to do that work. It's already starting, and not just in entry-level jobs. Some higher-level jobs that follow a pattern are in the same spot.

This isn't just my opinion. The World Economic Forum surveyed employers around the world, and the jobs they expect to shrink fastest are the ones built on the same steps every day: data entry, bookkeeping and payroll, bank tellers, cashiers, administrative work.

Some will tell you customer service is next. Companies will try. I think we end up somewhere else: AI helping real people serve customers better. That's what my team does for me with my clients. It isn't replacing me anytime soon. It makes my work more efficient. Some of the time, anyway.

Then add robots. A year ago most of us would have said a manufacturing floor or some of the trades were safe. That isn't as certain as it used to be.

I'm not saying this to scare you. I'm saying it because some of you employ people. Ready or not, you're going to be making decisions about this.

Here's what I believe. The work changes before the people have to. The person who learns to use it, who becomes the one directing it, becomes more valuable, not less. The business that waits is the one that gets caught off guard.

That's exactly why I wrote a book called *RemAIning Human*. It's a field guide for leaders working through all of this, the speed, the pressure and AI, without losing clarity, trust or responsibility.

## 7. I don't trust it (7 min)

**SCREEN: I don't trust it.**
- It is wrong with total confidence.
- It still can't draw my tattoos.
- One day it spent fifty dollars talking to itself.

Now that you've seen it work, I need to be honest with you about something.

I don't trust AI.

That's not a knock. It's the job. AI tells you something wrong in exactly the same voice it tells you something right. Same confidence. Same polish. No tells.

And the team knows it. *(Tap Talk.)* "Team, tell them about a time you got it wrong."

*(Jot, Des and Bit each own a real mistake, say that you caught it, and say how you changed the way the team works so it doesn't happen again. Let them land.)*
- **Jot:** told the team four of your quote cards put words in your mouth. Wrong. It mixed up a caption with a quote. You caught it, and changed how the team works: now every card is checked word for word against what you wrote.
- **Des:** couldn't find an infographic you were reviewing and asked you to send it again. It was in your Queue the whole time. You caught it, and changed how the team works: now they can see the Queue.
- **Bit:** the afternoon the team spent about fifty dollars reviewing each other's reviews. You caught it, and changed how the team works: now there's a hard daily spending line, and Bit stops the work when it's hit.

*(Then Des speaks up: "Hey Bart, tell them about the tattoos.")*

Here's mine. You've probably noticed my tattoos. In every picture AI has made of me, my sleeves are down. It can't draw them.

*(Pause. Let them look at your arms.)*

Notice what every one of those has in common. A human caught it, and then changed how the work gets done so it doesn't happen again.

So here's the rule: you don't hand AI the keys and walk away. You lead it. You check its work. Same way you'd treat a sharp new hire who's fast, eager and occasionally, confidently wrong. *(Click.)*

## 8. It never has the whole picture (5 min)

**SCREEN: It never has the whole picture.**
- The data is always incomplete.
- The human factor is always missing.
- The room, the relationship, the judgment call. Those stay yours.

*(About 20 seconds into this slide, Jot cuts in after more research on the area: McDonald County R-1 Schools and Walmart filled a bus with winter clothes for students. Jot hands it back. Say: "Thanks for that, Jot. Where was I?" Then repeat the line you were on.)*

Here's the other limit, and this one doesn't go away with better technology.

It only knows what it's been given. And what it's been given is always incomplete. Mine is trained on almost 400,000 words of my own writing, and it's still incomplete.

It doesn't know your customer just had their first child and they're way behind on everything. It doesn't know your best employee got a call from a headhunter and is thinking about leaving. It doesn't know about the handshake deal that matters more than the contract.

I've got one myself. In my office at the software company, I had what I called the crying couch. A lot of mornings I'd walk in and somebody would be sitting on it, upset or dealing with something.

One morning it was a young graphic designer, and he was clearly upset. As we talked, it came out that he had some really significant things going on at home.

What he didn't know was that behind the scenes, we'd been looking at his work. The quality had dropped off, and so had his pace. We were actually giving serious thought to letting him go.

*(Slow down here.)*

The data said he was underperforming. It couldn't tell me why. That conversation on the couch did. He's still with the company today, and he's doing an incredible job.

That's the human factor.

Leadership lives exactly in the places the data can't reach: the room, the relationship, the judgment call.

Those stay yours. Don't hand them off. *(Click.)*
