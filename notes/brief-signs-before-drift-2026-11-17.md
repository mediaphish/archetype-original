BRIEF: The Signs That Show Up Before Drift (journal post)

WHY THIS POST EXISTS
Most leaders find out something is wrong at the end: a resignation, a blowup, a missed number. By then the signs have been in the room for months. This post teaches the leader in the middle to see those signs early, while they are still small and still fixable. It serves the platform's purpose: helping people recognize the leader who stops seeing their people, and choose not to become that leader.

THESIS
Culture rarely breaks all at once. It goes quiet first. The early signs are small, ordinary, and easy to explain away, and they almost always show up in how honestly people talk to the leader before they show up in results. A leader who learns to notice them gets the one thing a late leader never has: time.

AUDIENCE
The leader in the middle: owners, managers and team leads of small and mid-sized businesses who are busy, producing, and assuming things are fine because nobody has said otherwise.

THE MATERIAL (source: Bart's original Culture Science notes on early warning signs, now retired from the ALI section)
Use these as the raw signs. Group and trim them; do not list all twelve. Write them as things a leader can see in a normal week.
1. Clarity gets sticky. People hesitate to ask questions, assume instead of confirm, repeat instructions back wrong, quietly reinterpret directions. (Condition: Clarity)
2. Conversation thins out. More text than talk, shorter messages, fewer clarifying questions, more tone misread. (Clarity, Voice)
3. People start guarding. They edit themselves, withhold small truths, choose the safer answer, watch before they contribute. Not fear yet. The step before it. (Voice)
4. Standards wobble. Missed deadlines, half follow-through, decisions that change depending on the day. (Consistency, Accountability)
5. Trust softens before it breaks. People rely on each other a little less, read intentions less generously, second-guess commitments. Reversible if caught here. (Trust, as a result)
6. Ownership turns into waiting. "Just tell me what to do." Less initiative, more waiting for permission, nobody stepping forward. (Growth: people trusted with real decisions)
7. The load lands on the same few. Stress spills over, energy drops, the dependable people quietly carry more until they don't. (Support)
8. Everyone is busy, nobody is pulling together. Meetings that end without clarity, people stepping on each other's roles, the leader correcting the same thing again. (Alignment)
9. The quiet goes all the way down. People stop volunteering ideas and stay silent even when things are breaking. This is the sign most leaders finally see, and it is the last one, not the first. (Commitment, as a result)

RECEIPTS (verify each before citing; quote only what you have read; no Wikipedia)
- Elizabeth Wolfe Morrison and Frances Milliken, "Organizational Silence: A Barrier to Change and Development in a Pluralistic World," Academy of Management Review 25, no. 4 (2000). Silence as a collective pattern that forms when people believe speaking up is futile or risky, often shaped by what managers believe and signal.
- James Detert and Amy Edmondson, "Implicit Voice Theories: Taken-for-Granted Rules of Self-Censorship at Work," Academy of Management Journal 54, no. 3 (2011). People hold back even with bosses who seem open, because of unspoken rules about when speaking up is safe.
- Amy Edmondson, The Fearless Organization (Wiley, 2018). How teams stop raising problems early, and what leaders do to make candor normal.
- Diane Vaughan, The Challenger Launch Decision (University of Chicago Press, 1996). "Normalization of deviance": small departures from the standard get explained away until they become the standard. Use as the frame for "easy to explain away."
- William Kahn, "Psychological Conditions of Personal Engagement and Disengagement at Work," Academy of Management Journal 33, no. 4 (1990). People withdraw parts of themselves from their work before they ever leave.
- Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter, The Truth About Burnout (Jossey-Bass, 1997). Burnout as a mismatch between people and the conditions of their work (workload, fairness, control), not a personal weakness. Supports the "load on the same few" sign.

LEADERSHIP THREAD (the turn the post builds to)
The signs are not a verdict on the team. They are a report on the conditions the leader has created. The first move is not a policy or a meeting about morale. It is the leader going first: asking a real question and waiting for the real answer, admitting a miss out loud, closing a loop that was left open, taking one decision back off the same few people. Small repairs, made early, made visibly.

GUARDRAILS
- Do not say ALI detects, tracks or measures these signs. They are things a leader can see, not survey items.
- Do not claim the signs always appear in the same order or predict anything "with accuracy." Use "often," "usually," "tends to."
- Do not use the retired condition names as labels (no "Communication Drift," "Stability Cracks," "psychological safety" as a named measure). Use plain language, and where a condition fits, use the current seven: Clarity, Consistency, Accountability, Voice, Growth, Support, Alignment.

SHAPE
Journal post, about 1,200 to 1,600 words. Open with a scene of a normal week where nothing looks wrong. Walk through the signs in the order they tend to appear, grouped, each with what it looks like and what it tells the leader. Close on the leadership thread.
