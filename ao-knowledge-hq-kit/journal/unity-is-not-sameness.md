The growth of humans is tied to being united with others who are not the same.

Most leaders would agree with that in principle and then build a team that violates it in practice. Unity is not sameness. That mix-up is costing you good people.

Leaders hear "unity" and reach for sameness. Same opinions in the room. Same way of solving problems. Same background, same pace, same risk tolerance. It feels like unity because it feels smooth. No friction, no pushback, everyone nodding along.

It is not unity. It is control wearing unity's name.

I heard a sermon on Ephesians 4 that framed this clearly. Unity is people rooted in the same purpose who are not the same. Different backgrounds, different gifts, different ways of seeing a problem, all moving toward one thing. The example given was two men who should have hated each other by every social rule of their time, now sharing a table because something bigger than their differences held them together. That is unity. Not one voice. One direction, carried by many different voices.

Now put that in a business.

A leader who mistakes unity for sameness starts flattening people. Hiring shifts toward fit instead of toward what the team is missing. Whoever agrees fastest gets rewarded, and whoever sees it differently gets quietly sidelined. Over time the team stops looking like a team and starts looking like an echo of the person running it. That gets called culture. It is a mirror.

Here is the cost. Every person on that team was likely brought in because of something specific. A gift, a skill, a way of thinking the business needed at the time. When a leader forces "my way" as the only way, that is not unity being built. It is the exact abilities that got those people hired going to waste. The operations mind gets told to stop questioning the vision. The relationship builder gets told to stick to the numbers. The person who sees six months out gets told to focus on this week. None of that is leadership. It is waste wearing a leader's title.

You do not go to a tattoo artist who does traditional work and ask for realism instead. That artist might fake his way through it, but the work will never be as good as what the realism specialist would have given you, and you will have wasted the one thing that made hiring that artist worth it in the first place. No business owner would make that mistake with a vendor. Leaders make it with their own team constantly. Someone gets hired for a specific skill, a specific way of seeing the business, and then the moment that approach differs from the leader's own, the pressure comes to do it "the leader's way" instead. The skill they were hired for sits unused while they spend their energy trying to think like someone they are not.

I want to be clear about something. I am not a "diversity is our strength" guy. Said on its own, that phrase treats difference as the goal instead of the byproduct. It is not. The goal is the mission. Difference only matters because it serves the mission better than sameness does.

Hire for demographic variety alone and stop there, and you can end up with a team that looks different in a photo and thinks in unison in the room. Nobody was actually brought in for a different way of solving problems, only a different look. That is not unity. It is optics wearing a buzzword.

A room full of people who think alike will move fast and miss what is coming. A room full of people who are unified in purpose but different in gifts will see around corners the leader alone never could.

That is the whole point. Unity does not erase what makes each person useful. It puts it to work.

Look at Uber under Travis Kalanick.

Kalanick co-founded the company in March 2009 and took over as full-time CEO in December 2010, after the app had already launched in San Francisco that May. From there the growth was relentless. New cities every few months, then new countries, then a valuation climbing into the tens of billions within a handful of years. Kalanick built that speed on purpose, and he built it in his own image. He hired for hustle and aggression, the same edge he carried himself. People who pushed back on the pace or the tactics did not last long around him. The culture rewarded whoever moved like Kalanick and quietly pushed out whoever thought differently about risk or restraint.

In February 2017, former engineer Susan Fowler published an account of harassment and dysfunction inside the company. The board brought in former Attorney General Eric Holder to investigate. His report confirmed what employees had been saying for months. More than twenty senior executives left in the aftermath. Uber's president, Jeff Jones, had already quit in March 2017, less than seven months into the job. Jones came from Target, where he had spent years running marketing, and Uber brought him in to fix the driver relationship and clean up the company's image. He tried. He did a public Q&A with drivers to hear them out directly. It went badly, and he cut it short after thirty minutes rather than keep dodging their questions about pay and fare cuts. When Uber announced it was hiring a chief operating officer, a move that would have boxed Jones out of the responsibilities he already had, he read the room correctly and left. His resignation statement did not hedge. He said the leadership and beliefs he had built his career on were inconsistent with what he saw and experienced at Uber, and he could not continue as president.

By June 2017, Uber's own investors forced Kalanick to resign as CEO.

Kalanick did not fail from a lack of talent around him. He failed because he built a team that mirrored him instead of a team that complemented him. Nobody with a different read on risk, a different sense of restraint, or a different way of treating people had enough standing left in the room to be heard. That is not unity. That is one voice amplified until it cost him the company.

Most of the time this failure never makes a headline at all. It just shows up as good news in every meeting, year after year, while the real numbers get worse where nobody outside the room can see them.

Before Alan Mulally arrived at Ford in September 2006, the company was on track to lose seventeen billion dollars that year. Every week the senior leaders met and reported their progress with a simple color code. Green for on track, yellow for at risk, red for a real problem. Every single chart came back green. Ford was bleeding out in public and every executive in the room said everything was fine, because saying otherwise had gotten people fired under the old regime. The truth had nowhere safe to land, so nobody told it. That is what isolation does. It does not just cost you honesty. It lets a lie sit unchallenged until it becomes the culture.

Mulally changed one thing first. He made it safe to be different in that room. The new Ford Edge, a crossover Ford needed to launch on schedule, had a suspension problem that threatened to delay production. When Mark Fields finally marked that chart red instead of hiding it behind green, Mulally stood up and applauded him in front of everyone. He thanked him. Then he asked the room how they could help. That single reaction told every leader in that building that disagreement, bad news, and a different read on reality would be rewarded instead of punished. Executives who used to protect themselves started bringing their real numbers, their real doubts, their real expertise, all of it pointed at one plan instead of one man's version of reality. Ford avoided the bankruptcy that took down its competitors and did it without a federal bailout.

What Mulally did in that room was not tolerance. He did not let the failing product line slide, and he did not pretend Fields' problem was smaller than it was. He held the standard and still made room for the truth to reach him. That is love. Not toxic empathy that excuses a bad result to keep the peace. Not looking away from a hard problem because naming it feels unkind. Love holds the standard with care.

This is the line between transformational leadership and servant leadership. Transformational leadership pushes people toward a vision. Servant leadership pushes people toward a vision too, but it puts the care back into the push. The vision does not get softer. The way you carry people to it does.

Love is not what most leaders think it is. It is not the gentleness that avoids conflict to stay comfortable. It is closer to fire. A real fire burns away what is not good and right and leaves what can stand. That is what Mulally's applause did in that room. It burned off the fear that had been protecting the lie, and it left the truth standing where everyone could finally use it.

Same kind of crisis, opposite instinct at the top. One leader built a team that echoed him, and the truth tried to reach him anyway, more than once. Fowler tried to deliver it through the company's own reporting system, and by her account HR protected the manager she reported and pushed her toward the door instead. Jones tried to deliver it by staying and pushing for change from inside, and the day Uber handed his own responsibilities to a COO search, he read that as the door closing on him too. That is what the model was built to do. It did not fail to produce truth-tellers. It made sure none of them kept enough standing in the room to be heard. The other leader built a team that told him the truth from inside the room, while there was still time to fix it, and the company came through it.

None of this means a leader should have no strong convictions or no clear way of doing things. A leader without conviction is not humble, that leader is absent.

At Midwestern Interactive, we had something we called the Midwestern Way. It was a published document. Everyone in the company had access to it. It was not static either. As the company grew, as our culture shifted, as the demands of the work changed, the Way changed with it. What never changed was what it served. The Way existed for the mission alone. It was not built to protect my preferences or make everyone operate like me. It was built to keep everyone pointed at the same target.

Inside that, people were free. As long as someone was working toward the mission of the company, or the mission of whatever project they were on, they used their own skills and their own way of getting there. Nobody had to think like me to belong. They had to be moving in the same direction I was.

That is the difference. A way that serves the mission holds a team together. A way that serves the leader holds a team hostage. The failure is not having a strong way. The failure is treating that way as the only shape the mission is allowed to take, so that every other gift in the room gets bent to match the leader instead of being put to work for the target.

This is where my own proof statement holds. If you grow people, you grow the organization. If you use people, you lose it. A leader who forces sameness is using people, whether that gets admitted or not. It asks people to shrink so the leader can stay comfortable. A leader who builds real unity is growing people, because it asks each of them to bring exactly what the leader does not have.

Unity was never meant to erase what makes each person useful. It was meant to hold that difference together and point it at one thing.

That is the whole point of where this started. The growth of humans is tied to being united with others who are not the same. It was true in Ephesians 4. It is true in your next hire, your next promotion, and the next person on your team who does not think like you.

Look at your own team. Do the people around you sound like you, or do they cover what you cannot see? One of those is an echo. The other is unity.
