## Scripture

**Micah 6:6–8 (ESV)**

## Reflection

Micah stacks the offers higher with each question. Burnt offerings, then thousands of rams, then rivers of oil, then finally the unthinkable, a firstborn child. Each one is bigger than the last, and each one is the wrong answer. The passage builds toward an escalation a leader would recognize immediately, the instinct to solve a debt of conscience by doing something dramatic enough to cover it.

The answer that actually comes is smaller than anything offered and far harder to fake. Do justice. Love mercy. Walk humbly. None of it produces a receipt. A leader can point to the rams and the oil. Nobody can point to humility the way they can point to a sacrifice.

That is precisely why this is the requirement and not the offering. A grand gesture can be staged once and referenced for years. Justice, mercy, and humility have to be lived out in the next decision, and the one after that, with no single moment that settles the account permanently.

Grace and mercy get treated in a lot of leadership circles as a reward a leader extends once the harder standards have been met. Micah reverses that. Mercy sits inside the requirement itself, next to justice, not after it. A leader does not earn the right to be merciful by first being tough enough. The two are asked for in the same breath.

September opens on this because it has to be said before anything else in this arc will hold. The rest of the month asks what mercy looks like in the hands of specific leaders, real and biblical. This is the baseline they are all being measured against.

## Practical Application

- Name one decision this week where doing justice and showing mercy could pull in different directions, and notice which one your instinct reaches for first.
- Choose the humbler, quieter response on purpose instead of the more visible one.
- Stop measuring this month by what got produced. Measure it by how people were treated along the way.

## Takeaways

- Mercy is named as a requirement in the same sentence as justice, not as something earned after the harder standard is met.
- A dramatic gesture is easier to offer, and easier to fake, than an ordinary posture held consistently.

## Closing Thought

What is required rarely looks as impressive as what was offered instead.
