## Scripture

**Proverbs 19:11 (ESV)**

## Reflection

The word glory is doing unexpected work in this proverb. Most leaders would place glory somewhere else entirely, in the decisive response, the correction delivered well, the standard visibly enforced. This proverb places it in the opposite direction, in the offense that gets overlooked rather than pursued.

That reordering is worth taking seriously rather than skimming past. Overlooking an offense sounds passive, even weak, next to confronting one. The proverb insists it's the opposite, that choosing not to pursue every offense available to you is itself a form of strength, and specifically a form connected to good sense rather than avoidance.

Good sense is the operative phrase here, because this isn't advocating for a leader who overlooks everything indiscriminately, unable to distinguish a pattern from a one-time lapse. The proverb pairs slow to anger with good sense, suggesting the overlooking described here is a judgment call, made by someone capable of anger who chooses restraint anyway, not someone incapable of noticing the offense in the first place.

Leaders accumulate opportunities to be offended constantly, in small doses, most of them genuinely minor. Every one of those moments is a decision point, whether small enough to release without comment or serious enough to require a response. A leader who pursues every available offense trains their team to walk carefully around them at all times. A leader who has genuinely mastered which offenses are worth overlooking creates room for people to be imperfect without treating every imperfection as an incident.

This is mercy stated as strength rather than softness, glory rather than a concession. The leader who can overlook an offense and mean it, not suppress it while quietly keeping score, has something the proverb is willing to call glorious.

## Practical Application

- Identify one recent offense you're still holding onto that good sense would say to release.
- Practice the distinction between an offense worth addressing and one worth genuinely overlooking.
- Notice whether you're overlooking things outwardly while still keeping score internally, and be honest about the difference.

## Takeaways

- Overlooking an offense is named as glory in this proverb, not as weakness or avoidance.
- Good sense distinguishes what's worth overlooking from what requires a response, rather than overlooking everything indiscriminately.

## Closing Thought

The glory here belongs to the offense that was never pursued.
