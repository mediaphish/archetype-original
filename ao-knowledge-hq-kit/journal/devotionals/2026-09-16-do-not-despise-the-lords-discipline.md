## Scripture

**Proverbs 3:11–12 (ESV)**

## Reflection

This proverb is addressed to the one receiving correction, not the one giving it, which makes it a useful companion to yesterday's passage rather than a repeat of it. Do not despise the discipline. Do not grow weary of the reproof. A leader spends most of their time thinking about how to correct others well. This proverb asks a different question, how does a leader receive correction when it comes to them.

Despising discipline and being weary of reproof are two different failures, and most leaders are more prone to one than the other. Despising it looks like resentment, treating the correction as an insult rather than information. Weariness looks like exhaustion, having been corrected often enough that each new instance feels like one more thing to endure rather than something worth actually hearing.

The comparison to a father delighting in a son reframes what correction is actually doing. It isn't a father tolerating a disappointing son and grudgingly stepping in. It's specifically the son in whom he delights who receives the reproof. Correction aimed at someone a leader delights in looks completely different than correction aimed at someone they've merely tolerated, and the person receiving it can usually tell the difference.

A leader who wants to extend mercy well to others has to be someone who can receive correction without despising it or growing weary of it first. It's difficult to model grace toward someone else's failure while treating your own correction as either an insult or a burden. The posture required to receive discipline well and the posture required to extend mercy well are closer to the same skill than they first appear.

This proverb sits at the center of the week for a reason. Everything on either side of it, giving correction rooted in love, receiving grace that still holds a standard, depends on a leader who has made peace with being corrected themselves.

## Practical Application

- Notice your own instinct the next time you're corrected, whether it leans toward resentment or exhaustion.
- Receive one piece of feedback this week as evidence someone delights in your growth, not as an insult to absorb.
- Practice extending the same grace to your own correction that you'd want to extend to someone else's failure.

## Takeaways

- Despising discipline and growing weary of it are two different failures, and most leaders lean toward one more than the other.
- Correction from someone who delights in you looks and feels different than correction from someone who merely tolerates you.

## Closing Thought

The son being reproved here is the son being delighted in, not the son being merely tolerated.
