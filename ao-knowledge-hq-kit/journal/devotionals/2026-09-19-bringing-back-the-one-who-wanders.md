## Scripture

**James 5:19–20 (ESV)**

## Reflection

James describes wandering, not open rebellion. That distinction matters. Most people who drift from what they know to be right don't announce it or defend it. They simply wander, one small compromise away at a time, often without a clear moment where they decided to leave anything behind. The correction this passage calls for isn't aimed at confronting a declared enemy. It's aimed at going after someone who's drifted further than they meant to.

Someone brings him back is doing significant work in this sentence. James doesn't say someone points out that the person wandered, or someone documents the wandering for the record. Someone brings him back, an active pursuit rather than a passive observation. Mercy here looks like effort spent going after a person, not just noticing correctly that they'd left.

The stakes James attaches to this are considerable, saving a soul from death and covering a multitude of sins. That's a serious weight to place on what could easily be mistaken for a minor act of intervention. A leader who goes to the trouble of pursuing someone who's wandered, rather than letting the drift continue unaddressed, is doing something James treats as consequential rather than optional.

This is different from restoring someone already caught, which the arc addressed back in the first week. Wandering happens gradually and often invisibly, which means it requires a leader who's actually paying attention, not just responding once a failure becomes obvious. Mercy that only activates after a clear, visible failure misses everyone who's quietly drifting in the meantime.

A leader extending grace and mercy this month has to include this kind of attentiveness, not just readiness to respond well once someone's failure is undeniable, but the willingness to notice a slow wander and go after it before it becomes something worse.

## Practical Application

- Identify someone who seems to be drifting gradually rather than someone who's already failed outright.
- Go to them directly rather than simply noting the drift and waiting to see where it leads.
- Treat the pursuit as consequential, not as an optional intervention you might get to eventually.

## Takeaways

- Wandering is gradual and often invisible, which requires attentiveness a leader has to choose to bring.
- Bringing someone back is an active pursuit, not a passive observation that they've drifted.

## Closing Thought

Someone has to go after the wandering. It rarely corrects itself.
