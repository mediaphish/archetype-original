## Scripture

**James 4:13–15 (ESV)**

## Reflection

Nothing here is an argument against planning. The plan described is a good one. A town, a timeline, a line of business, an expected profit. Any leader would recognize it as competent thinking rather than recklessness.

What James names is the certainty attached to it. We will go, we will spend a year, we will trade and make a profit. Those verbs assume a future that has not been handed over yet. A leader can hold a detailed plan and still hold it as a request rather than a guarantee.

If the Lord wills is not a phrase to append to the end of a strategy document. It changes what happens when the plan breaks. Plans held as guarantees produce blame when they fail, usually aimed at whoever was closest to the failure. Plans held as requests produce adjustment.

Time is entrusted under real conditions, and no leader owns next year. Treating it as owned inventory is what leads to overcommitting, promising what cannot be promised, and spending people to protect a timeline that was always a guess. Stewardship here means being honest about what is actually in hand.

A mist that appears for a little time is not a reason to plan less. It is a reason to plan honestly, leave room in commitments, and hold the calendar with a lighter grip than the planning process usually invites.

## Practical Application

- Find one commitment you have stated as a certainty that was really a forecast, and correct the record with the people affected.
- Build margin into the next plan you own, deliberately rather than as a cushion you hide.
- Listen for whether your team hears your plans as guarantees, and tell them plainly if they do.

## Takeaways

- The issue is not the plan. It is the certainty attached to a future that was never guaranteed.
- Plans held as guarantees produce blame when they break, while plans held as requests produce adjustment.

## Closing Thought

The plan was competent. The certainty was the problem.
