## Scripture

**Psalm 10:13 (ESV)**

## Reflection

The psalmist does not ask why the wicked does wrong. He asks something more specific: why does the wicked renounce God and say in his heart, you will not call me to account. The question assumes a very particular kind of reasoning, one built entirely around the expectation of an audit that will never come.

This is the clearest statement in the whole psalm of what avoiding accountability actually requires. Not just doing wrong, and not even fully denying God exists, though the wicked man says that too elsewhere in the psalm. The specific move here is narrower and more useful for a leader to recognize: deciding, as a settled premise, that no one will ever call this to account. Once that premise is in place, everything else follows from it.

Leaders rarely arrive at this premise through an argument. It accumulates through a long run of decisions that were never actually reviewed. A choice goes unquestioned once, then again, then enough times that the leader starts operating as though review itself is no longer a live possibility. The internal sentence is never spoken out loud, the way the psalm renders it. It just becomes the operating assumption behind everything that follows.

What makes the psalmist's question sharp is that he treats this as a decision, not a discovery. Renounce is an active verb. The wicked man is not simply mistaken about whether he will be called to account. He has renounced the idea, pushed it away, chosen not to carry it. That is a different problem than ignorance, and it requires a different response than more information.

The corrective is not proving to a leader that accountability exists. Most leaders already know that. The corrective is refusing to let that premise, you will not call me to account, take root as a working assumption, even quietly, even about the decisions that seem smallest.

## Practical Application

- Ask yourself directly whether any part of your leadership currently operates on the unspoken assumption that it will never be reviewed.
- Identify the pattern of small, unquestioned decisions that led to that assumption, rather than treating it as a single moment of choice.
- Actively invite review into the areas where you have quietly stopped expecting it.

## Takeaways

- Avoiding accountability is not primarily a belief problem. It is a decision, renounced and settled, that gets treated afterward as though it were simply true.
- The assumption that something will never be reviewed usually accumulates through a long run of unquestioned decisions, not a single argument.

## Closing Thought

He did not discover that no one was watching. He decided it, and then he built on it.
