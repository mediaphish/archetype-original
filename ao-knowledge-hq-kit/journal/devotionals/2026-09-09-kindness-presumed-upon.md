## Scripture

**Romans 2:1–4 (ESV)**

## Reflection

Paul sets up two options and rejects both. The first is assuming judgment simply won't apply to you, that somehow the standard you enforce on others has a personal exemption built in for the enforcer. The second, introduced by that "or," is subtler and more common: noticing that judgment hasn't landed yet, and mistaking the delay for permission. Neither option survives the passage. The kindness was never the absence of a standard. It was time to meet one.

The leader Paul describes here isn't a stranger to the standard. He's the judge, actively passing judgment on others for practicing the very things he does himself. That's a specific and uncomfortable target, not a general call to humility but a direct address to someone holding others accountable for a failure they share. A leader can build an entire reputation on enforcing a standard while quietly exempting their own version of the same behavior from the scrutiny they apply to everyone else.

What keeps that arrangement running is the second failure, presuming on kindness. God's patience toward this leader hasn't run out yet, and the leader reads that as evidence there's nothing to worry about, rather than evidence there's still time to change. A leader who has been shown grace by their own superiors, their board, their spouse, can fall into the identical trap, treating ongoing patience as proof the issue was never serious rather than as a window still open.

Mercy extended to a leader was never meant to function as cover for continuing to judge others for the same thing. That's the trap this passage names specifically, not just presuming on kindness in general, but presuming on it while simultaneously condemning someone else for the shared failure. The hypocrisy and the presumption are not two separate problems. They're the same posture, described from two angles.

A leader reading this seriously has to ask both questions Paul asks, not just whether they're presuming on patience, but whether they're judging someone else for something they haven't actually stopped doing themselves.

## Practical Application

- Name one standard you actively enforce on your team that you personally fall short of in some form.
- Ask honestly whether you've read your own unresolved consequences as safety rather than as time still available to change.
- Extend the same patience you've been shown to someone you've been judging for a shared failure.

## Takeaways

- Judging someone for a failure you share yourself is condemned specifically, not judgment in general.
- Ongoing patience is time to change, not evidence the standard doesn't actually apply.

## Closing Thought

The kindness was never the absence of a standard. It was time to meet one.
