## Scripture

**Hebrews 12:5–6 (ESV)**

## Reflection

This passage ties discipline directly to love, not as an exception to it. The Lord disciplines the one he loves. Not despite loving them. Because of it. That ordering runs against the instinct most leaders carry, which treats correction and affection as competing signals, so that the more a leader cares about someone, the more reluctant they become to actually correct them.

Discipline without love produces something else entirely, punishment aimed at the offender rather than restoration aimed at the person. This passage isn't describing that. It's describing a father who reproves specifically because the relationship matters enough to risk the discomfort of correction. Withholding discipline out of a desire to be liked isn't more loving. It's often just more comfortable for the one avoiding it.

A leader who has confused mercy with permissiveness will find this passage uncomfortable, because it names discipline as evidence of love rather than an interruption of it. Mercy shown to someone doesn't require pretending the correction isn't necessary. Grace and rebuke are not sequential, one following an exhausted supply of the other. They are the same act described from two directions.

Being weary when reproved is named here as a real temptation, not a hypothetical one. Correction is exhausting to receive and often more exhausting to give well. A leader who grows weary of correcting people they genuinely care about is at risk of quietly reclassifying that weariness as wisdom, deciding the correction wasn't worth the cost when the actual issue is fatigue.

The chastisement described here belongs specifically to sons, to people who are received, not discarded. A leader correcting someone they've already written off isn't doing what this passage describes. The discipline that flows from love only makes sense aimed at someone the leader still intends to keep.

## Practical Application

- Notice a correction you've been avoiding because you didn't want the discomfort of giving it.
- Separate genuine weariness with the process of correcting someone from a decision that the correction isn't warranted.
- Confirm, honestly, whether you still intend to keep the person you're correcting, or have quietly written them off.

## Takeaways

- Discipline is named as evidence of love in this passage, not a departure from it.
- Weariness with giving correction is a real temptation, and it can get mistaken for wisdom.

## Closing Thought

The discipline was never separate from the love. It was one of the ways the love showed up.
