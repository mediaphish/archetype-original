## Scripture

**2 Corinthians 8:18–21 (ESV)**

## Reflection

Paul is managing a significant sum of money, an offering collected from several churches for believers in Jerusalem, and he does something worth noticing before any question of misconduct has been raised. He arranges for a second, independently appointed leader to travel with him and oversee the funds jointly. No one accused Paul of anything. He built in accountability before it was ever demanded.

This week has traced accountability for what a leader was given from several directions: the weight tied to what was entrusted, the difference between preserving and producing, the standard held even without oversight, the posture of a steward rather than an owner, the responsibility to use a gift, and the discipline of guarding what was inherited. This closing passage adds a different move entirely. Paul does not simply wait to be asked for an account. He structures his leadership so the account is already visible before anyone has to ask.

Leaders often treat accountability as something that happens to them, an audit, a question, a summons. Paul treats it as something a leader builds. He could have managed the funds alone and trusted his own integrity to carry the day. Instead he chose a structure that made his integrity checkable by someone other than himself, on purpose, in advance.

Servant leadership does not wait for suspicion to arrive before becoming transparent. It aims at what is honorable in full view, not just in private conviction. A leader who welcomes scrutiny before it is requested has understood something the rest of this week has been building toward: accountability for what you were given is not fully met by being faithful in private. It is met by making that faithfulness visible enough for someone else to confirm it.

## Practical Application

- Identify one area where you are the only person who can see how something entrusted to you is actually being handled.
- Invite someone else into visibility on it this week, before any question has been raised.
- Treat that invitation as strength, not as an admission that something is wrong.

## Takeaways

- Paul built accountability into his leadership before anyone questioned him, appointing a second leader to oversee the funds jointly.
- Accountability for what you were given is not fully met by being faithful in private; it is met by making that faithfulness visible enough for someone else to confirm it.

## Closing Thought

The most credible leaders are not the ones who insist they can be trusted. They are the ones who make their trustworthiness checkable.
