# Twenty Points Apart

Gallup asked leaders to rate themselves on seven leadership competencies. Then they asked the managers who work for those leaders to rate the same people on the same seven things.

On six of the seven, the managers' ratings came in at least twenty percentage points below what the leaders gave themselves.

The lowest-scoring competency of all, for both groups, was creating accountability. Fewer than half of leaders called themselves outstanding at it. Their managers were harsher still. And among the 30% of managers who did say their leader was exceptional at holding everyone responsible for performance, engagement ran at 51%. For everyone else it ran at 17%. Three times the difference, on one competency.

Most of the coverage I have seen treats the twenty-point spread as a self-awareness problem. Leaders overrate themselves. Humbling, but fixable with better feedback.

I do not think that is what the number is measuring.

## Two different questions

A leader rating their own accountability is answering one question: do I hold a standard? They are scoring their intent, and their intent is usually good. They meant the deadline. They meant the expectation. They can remember setting it.

A manager rating that same leader is answering an entirely different question: what happens around here when the standard slips? They are not scoring intent. They cannot see intent. They are scoring the pattern of what actually gets tolerated.

Those two questions produce different numbers because they are about different things. The gap is not error. The gap is the finding.

I wrote about this in [The 7 Conditions: Clarity](/journal/ali-series-clarity-what-actually-lands), and the opening line of that piece is the whole argument: most leaders are certain they are clear, and their teams are living inside something different. Not because anyone is lying. Because clarity is not what you said. Clarity is what landed.

Accountability works the same way. It is not what you declared. It is what your team has watched you enforce.

## What twenty points looks like on a Tuesday

None of this shows up as a dramatic failure. It shows up small.

A leader sets a deadline and means it. The date arrives, the work is not done, and there is a reason. A good one. The leader says nothing, because saying something in that moment would feel petty. The deadline moves. Nobody logs it.

The same leader asks for candid feedback. Someone gives it. The leader gets a little quiet, a little clipped, and moves the meeting along. Nothing is said about it afterward. The team files it away.

A standard is announced in January. By April nobody has asked about it since February, and the work has quietly reorganized itself around what does get asked about.

The leader would score themselves well on all three. They set the deadline. They invited the feedback. They named the standard. Every one of those is true.

Their team is scoring something else: the deadline moved and nothing happened, honesty cost something, and the standard was real for six weeks. That is the twenty points. It is not a difference of opinion about the same events. It is two people looking at different evidence and reporting accurately.

## Why this one competency is the worst

It makes sense that accountability scores lowest of the seven. It is the only one that requires a leader to do something uncomfortable in a specific moment, in front of someone, at a cost to the mood in the room.

Every other competency can be satisfied with preparation. You can write a clear strategy at your desk. You can plan a communication in advance. Accountability cannot be prepared. It happens when the thing you said would matter turns out not to have happened, and you decide, in about four seconds, whether to name it.

The leaders who score well on this are not tougher than the ones who do not. They have usually just made the decision once, in advance, so that they are not renegotiating it in the moment while a person they like looks at them.

That is a design question, not a character question, which is the argument in [Accountability Is a Design Problem](/journal/accountability-is-a-design-problem). Accountability is not a personality trait. It is a design choice. Where it is missing, it is usually missing because nobody built anything to hold it.

## The part worth acting on

If you lead people, you already have a number in your head for how accountable you are. So does everyone who works for you, and Gallup's research says the two numbers are about twenty points apart.

You do not need a survey to close that. You need one honest question, asked of someone who has watched you: what have you seen slide?

Not "am I holding people accountable." That asks about intent, and it is the question the leader already answered generously. The other one asks about evidence, and the evidence is what your team has been living in the whole time.

The twenty points do not close because leaders get more self-aware. They close when what a leader tolerates stops disagreeing with what they said.
