# The Pipeline Isn't Draining. It's Refusing to Fill.

Seventy-one percent of leaders report increased stress right now. Forty percent of them have considered leaving their roles because of it. Development Dimensions International's (DDI) [2026 Global Leadership Forecast](https://www.ddi.com/blog/leadership-trends-2026) confirms those numbers this year, and most people are reading them as a wellness story.

They are not measuring wellness. They are measuring the health of the leadership pipeline, and the pipeline is close to breaking.

I've already written about the mechanism behind this. In [Toxic Empathy](/journal/toxic-empathy), I argued that most leaders don't lose their team all at once. They lose it slowly, one avoided conversation at a time, while convincing themselves they are being supportive. The standard doesn't disappear overnight. It softens. And the work that standard used to hold in place doesn't go away either. It moves, onto whoever is left still carrying it.

That piece was about what happens to the team when a leader avoids the cost of leading. This piece is about what happens to the leader.

In [The Cost of Calm](/journal/the-cost-of-calm), I wrote about thirty years of staying composed in chaos, and how being calm doesn't mean being healthy. I became so used to carrying pressure that I stopped noticing what it was costing me until my body forced the issue. I wasn't high-performing anymore. I was over-performing under constant strain, and I didn't have a name for it until it had already done damage.

Put those two pieces together and you get the shape of what's happening across leadership right now, at scale. A leader has a finite capacity to absorb pressure. When that capacity runs out, the pressure doesn't disappear. It goes somewhere. Sometimes it goes down, onto the team, which is the toxic empathy pattern. Sometimes it goes inward, onto the leader's own health, which is the cost of calm pattern. Right now the data says it's going both places at once.

DDI's research found that 77% of Chief Human Resources Officers (CHROs) lack confidence in their bench strength for critical roles, and only 19% of managers report having strong delegation skills, the exact skill that helps mitigate burnout in the first place. The layer that was supposed to develop the next generation of leaders is too overloaded to develop anyone, including itself.

I named this exact structure in [The Producer-Leader Paradox](/journal/the-producer-leader-paradox): a leader forced to keep producing at full speed cannot develop full speed at the same time. That paradox used to describe individual managers stuck between output and leadership. The DDI numbers say it has become the condition of an entire layer of leadership, everywhere at once.

Here's the part almost nobody is connecting to any of this. The generation watching from below has already made its decision, but not the decision most people assume. Only 6% of Gen Z and millennials say achieving a leadership position is their primary career goal, according to [Deloitte's 2026 Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey](https://www.deloitte.com/global/en/issues/work/genz-millennial-survey.html). That is not rejection. Most of them, 76% of Gen Z and 67% of millennials, say they want to reach senior or executive leadership eventually. They are not turning down leadership because they lack ambition. They are refusing it under current conditions. The same survey found the most commonly cited barriers to pursuing leadership are stress and burnout, excessive responsibility, and concerns about work-life balance.

That isn't a hiring problem. It's a conclusion, drawn correctly, from what they were shown.

This is where pipeline collapse actually starts. Not with the leader who quits this year. With the person three years out who was never going to raise their hand for the job, because the cost was visible before they ever applied.

The fix isn't a wellness program bolted onto an unchanged structure. A leader who is out of capacity does not need a meditation app. They need less weight on them, and a system underneath them that doesn't require personal sacrifice to hold the line, the same argument I made when I wrote that servant leadership stops being fragile once it's built into systems and rituals instead of resting on one person's willpower.

The leaders reporting stress this year are not weak. They are carrying a design flaw nobody built a system to catch. The 6% figure is what happens when that flaw goes unaddressed long enough for the next generation to notice it before you do.
