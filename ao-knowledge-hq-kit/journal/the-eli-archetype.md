*The Archetype Series, Entry Fourteen*

Samuel thought the old man was calling him.

The boy slept in the temple at Shiloh, a few steps from the ark of God. In the years before Israel had a king, Shiloh was where the whole nation came to worship, because the ark was kept there, and Israel believed the presence of God himself rested above that sacred chest. Every year families traveled up from their towns to offer their sacrifices before it. Samuel had lived there since he was small. His mother, Hannah, had begged God for a son and promised to give him back, and when the boy was old enough to leave her, she kept her word. She brought him to Shiloh and left him in the care of the man who ran it.

That man was Eli. He was the priest who stood at Shiloh's altar and offered the people's sacrifices to God on their behalf, and he was also Israel's judge, a post he held for forty years in all, which in a nation with no king made him the closest thing it had to a ruler. By now he was very old. His eyes had grown so dim he could not see, and he slept in his own place nearby, so when a voice in the dark said Samuel's name, the boy got up and ran to the only person who could have been calling him. "Here I am, for you called me."

Eli had not called, and he sent the boy back to bed. It happened a second time, and Eli sent him back again. In those days, the text says, "the word of the LORD was rare," and God seldom spoke to anyone. The third time the boy came running, the old man understood what Samuel could not. The voice was God's. Eli told him to go lie down, and if it came again, to answer it this way: "Speak, LORD, for your servant hears."

The voice came again, and what it said was about Eli.

Eli had two grown sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who served as priests at Shiloh under their father, and the people who came there to worship knew what kind of priests they were. They helped themselves to the offerings and slept with the women who served at the temple door. God told Samuel he was about to bring judgment on Eli's house, his whole family line, forever, "for the iniquity that he knew, because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them."

Samuel lay there until morning, and then he got up and opened the doors of the house of the LORD as if it were any other day, because he was afraid to tell Eli what he had heard. Eli would not let him carry it alone. "What was it that he told you? Do not hide it from me." So the boy told him everything, and the old man sat and listened while the child he had raised described the end of his family. When Samuel finished, Eli said: "It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him."

Read that once and it sounds like humility, an old priest hearing the worst news of his life and bowing to it. Read it again knowing what came next, because his answer to the verdict was a sentence, and the sentence turned out to be all there was. Hophni and Phinehas stayed at the altar. After that night the text never shows Eli calling them in again, removing them or changing anything at all. He agreed with God out loud and went back to his seat, as if agreeing with the verdict were the same thing as answering it.

[The last entry](/journal/the-josiah-archetype) in this series was about Josiah, a king who reigned in Jerusalem centuries after Eli. When a lost book of God's law was read aloud to him, he learned that judgment on his kingdom was already set and that his obedience would not change it, and he carried out every line of the book anyway. Eli asked for his hard word too. He pressed a frightened boy for it, and when he got it he took it in and did nothing with any of it.

Eli already knew why. He had been hearing about his sons for years, and he never held them accountable.

**What he already knew**

Scripture introduces Eli's sons in one blunt sentence: "Now the sons of Eli were worthless men. They did not know the LORD." Then it shows what that looked like to a family who came to Shiloh to worship.

A man would bring an animal to be sacrificed, and part of the meat was set boiling in a pot so his household could eat it there together, a meal shared in God's presence. While it cooked, the priest's servant would walk up "with a three-pronged fork in his hand" and drive it into the pot, and "all that the fork brought up the priest would take for himself." The family sat down to whatever the fork had left them.

That was the lesser abuse. The worse one took from God before it took from the worshiper. The fat of every animal was the LORD's portion, and it was meant to be burned on the altar before anyone else was served. Eli's sons would not wait for it. Their servant demanded raw meat for the priest to roast while the fat was still unburned, and if the man who brought the animal pushed back and said, "Let them burn the fat first, and then take as much as you wish," the servant answered him: "No, you must give it now, and if not, I will take it by force."

So ordinary people walked up to Shiloh to worship and walked home shaken down by the very men who were supposed to stand between them and God, and the text gives its verdict in a single line: "the men treated the offering of the LORD with contempt." It went beyond the meat. Eli's sons also slept with the women who served at the entrance to the tent of meeting. That was Scripture's name for the sacred place at the heart of Shiloh, where Israel came to meet with God.

Then comes the verse this whole archetype turns on. "Now Eli was very old, and he kept hearing all that his sons were doing to all Israel." He kept hearing. The reports came in from many mouths over a long stretch of time, and still he never took action against them.

What he did instead was talk to them, and every word he said was true. "Why do you do such things? For I hear of your evil dealings from all these people. No, my sons; it is no good report that I hear the people of the LORD spreading abroad." He named what they were doing and told them who was talking about it, and then he warned them how serious it had become: if a man sins against another man, God can mediate for him, but if a man sins against the LORD, who can intercede for him?

"But they would not listen to the voice of their father."

Most leaders get this far, because talking is the easy part. What Eli never did was the part that costs something. He never restrained them, and that is the very word God would use against him. His sons kept taking the offerings by force and kept lying with the women who served at the tent of meeting, right up to the day they died, because a rebuke with nothing behind it leaves the behavior just where it found it.

That conversation is the last thing the text records Eli doing about his sons, and the authority to do more was his. If anyone in Israel could take a priest away from the altar, it was the senior priest who was also the nation's judge. Restraint is what that authority was for once talking had failed, and he used his voice and left the authority in its sheath.

Many leaders have sat in some version of that room. Someone on the team is doing real damage, the reports keep coming, and eventually the leader sits that person down and says all the right things, clearly and even firmly. Then the person goes back to work, and the work goes on as before, because the conversation was the whole of the response.

Eli had already been told, in plainer words than he ever used with his sons, where his own share of it lay. Before Samuel's night, God had sent him a messenger.

**Fattening yourselves**

The text never gives the messenger a name. It calls him only "a man of God," a prophet sent to speak God's words, and he came to Eli with a reminder before he came with a charge. God had chosen Eli's family out of all the tribes of Israel to serve at his altar. Then the man of God asked the question: "Why then do you scorn my sacrifices and my offerings that I commanded for my dwelling, and honor your sons above me by fattening yourselves on the choicest parts of every offering of my people Israel?"

Look at the pronoun. Yourselves. The man of God could have said your sons, and he said you. The charge is plural, and Eli stands inside it.

The sentence on his family was total. "For those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me shall be lightly esteemed." The priesthood would pass out of Eli's line. No man in his house would live to grow old, and whoever was left would come begging for a piece of silver or a loaf of bread. As the sign that all of it was on its way, his two sons would go first: "both of them shall die on the same day."

That charge explains Eli's inaction better than weakness does. Tolerance usually pays the leader something. The top performer who mistreats people still brings in the number the leader is paid on. The person who should have been moved out also spares the leader a hard fight, and the quiet that follows feels a lot like peace. Before you judge Eli, look at what his tolerance was feeding, and then look at what yours is.

What makes him hard to dismiss is how good he was at the rest of the job. He raised the boy who became the prophet all Israel trusted.

**The good mentor**

The story keeps setting Eli's two households side by side, almost line for line. His sons take the meat by force, and "the boy Samuel grew in the presence of the LORD." Eli rebukes his sons and they ignore him, and the boy Samuel "continued to grow both in stature and in favor with the LORD and also with man." On the night the voice came, Samuel "did not yet know the LORD." Eli did, and he taught the boy how to answer. He could teach someone else's son to listen to God, and when his own sons refused to listen to him, he had nothing to give them past the words he had already spent.

One verse in the middle of all this cuts two ways. Right after the sons refuse their father, the text adds: "for it was the will of the LORD to put them to death." God's purpose is in this story, and Eli's responsibility is in it too, because a chapter later God still lays the charge at Eli's feet: "he did not restrain them." The text holds both at once, and so should we.

That is what keeps Eli from being a villain. He was a faithful teacher to a boy who was not his own. Most leaders who fail this way are good at the job everywhere else, and the good they do in every other room becomes the reason nobody, the leader included, notices that in one room he has only ever talked.

The bill came due on a single day, when Israel went to war.

**The day of the battle**

Israel's enemy in those years was the Philistines. After losing the first battle, the elders sent to Shiloh for the ark, "that it may come among us and save us from the power of our enemies," as if the holiest object in Israel were a charm you could carry into a fight. Eli's sons came with it. Thirty thousand Israelite foot soldiers fell that day. The ark of God was captured, and Hophni and Phinehas both died, on the same day, exactly as the man of God had said.

Back at Shiloh, Eli was sitting on his seat by the road, "for his heart trembled for the ark of God." He was ninety-eight and blind. A runner came from the battle line and told him: "Israel has fled before the Philistines, and there has also been a great defeat among the people. Your two sons also, Hophni and Phinehas, are dead, and the ark of God has been captured."

"As soon as he mentioned the ark of God, Eli fell over backward from his seat by the side of the gate, and his neck was broken and he died, for the man was old and heavy. He had judged Israel forty years."

Old and heavy. The man of God had charged him with fattening himself on what his sons took by force, and the text names his weight as part of why the fall killed him.

The news kept moving through his house after he was gone. His daughter-in-law, Phinehas's wife, was pregnant and close to giving birth, and when she heard that the ark was taken and that her husband and father-in-law were dead, she went into labor and died bringing the child into the world. Before she died, she named the boy Ichabod, "saying, 'The glory has departed from Israel!'"

A version of the same story played out in 2001, in a football building in Pennsylvania, with far more grey in it.

**The coach who handed it off**

Joe Paterno had been the head football coach at Penn State University since 1966, and Jerry Sandusky had spent almost thirty years on his staff, most of them as his defensive coordinator. Sandusky retired in 1999, but he kept access to the Lasch Football Building, where the program had its offices.

On the night of February 9, 2001, Mike McQueary, a graduate student working as an assistant on Paterno's staff, walked into the Lasch Building and saw Sandusky with a young boy in the showers. The date comes from the investigation Penn State's board of trustees commissioned a decade later, after Sandusky was charged with abusing boys, when they hired Louis Freeh, the former director of the FBI, to find out what the university had known. Before Freeh's team ever looked, prosecutors had already put the case in front of ordinary citizens who sat and listened to the evidence before anyone was charged, and the report that this grand jury issued in 2011 placed that night in March 2002. Freeh's team turned up critical emails from 2001 and confirmed through a separate forensic review that it happened more than a year earlier.

The next morning was a Saturday. McQueary called Paterno and went to his house to tell him. Paterno later described to the grand jury what he heard that morning: "It was a sexual nature." Then he waited. In that same testimony, he explained why: "I ordinarily would have called people right away, but it was a Saturday morning and I didn't want to interfere with their weekends." A year later, in his last interview, he gave Sally Jenkins of the Washington Post a different reason. "So I sat around. It was a Saturday. Waited till Sunday because I wanted to make sure I knew what I was doing. And then I called my superiors and I said, 'Hey, we got a problem, I think. Would you guys look into it?'"

What Paterno did next is what a great many men his age and in his position would have done. He took what he had heard to his boss, athletic director Tim Curley, and handed it to people he believed were better placed to act on it. That is a fair thing to do. Handing a hard problem to the person best placed to handle it is how an organization is supposed to work, and most leaders do it every week. The trouble is that a phone call is still words, and some of this was still his to act on after the call ended.

Curley was not alone in what came next. Gary Schultz, who oversaw the university police, and Graham Spanier, the university's president, were soon part of the decision as well. According to the Freeh report, on February 25, 2001, the three of them agreed on a plan that included reporting Sandusky to the state Department of Public Welfare, the agency responsible for protecting children. Two days later, on February 27, Curley emailed the other two that he had changed his mind "after giving it more thought and talking it over with Joe yesterday." The report concluded that the only known factor between the plan and the reversal was Paterno's conversation with Curley.

The report also pointed to the part that had stayed with Paterno the whole time. As head coach, it said, he had the authority to establish permissible uses of his football facilities. The program and the coaches who worked in it were his to direct. "At the very least, Mr. Paterno could have alerted the entire football staff, in order to prevent Sandusky from bringing another child into the Lasch Building." The report found that he did not, and Sandusky kept his access to the football facilities for another decade, until his arrest.

The charges against Sandusky became public in November 2011. On November 9, Paterno announced that he would retire at the end of the season and released a statement: "This is a tragedy. It is one of the great sorrows of my life. With the benefit of hindsight, I wish I had done more." That night, in his forty-sixth season, the trustees fired him by phone, and in a report the following March they explained why: "his decision to do his minimum legal duty and not to do more to follow up constituted a failure of leadership by Coach Paterno."

The limits belong in plain view. Paterno met his legal duty to report, and Frank Noonan, the Pennsylvania State Police commissioner, said so at the time. The attorney general said Paterno was not a target, and he was never charged with anything. According to Jenkins, Paterno's lawyer said Sandusky's access to the building after he retired was a contractual arrangement Paterno did not grant. Paterno always said McQueary never gave him the specific details, and his family commissioned its own review, which disputes the Freeh report and argues that it misread Curley's email. I am not resting anything on the disputed parts. The undisputed facts are enough: he was told something of a sexual nature had happened to a boy in his building, he passed it up the chain, and he did not take it to the police.

The same commissioner who said Paterno had met his legal duty also said this: "I think you have a moral responsibility to call us."

Eli's case is plain. Paterno's is greyer, and he only just crosses the line. That is what makes him useful, because very few leaders will ever sit where Eli sat, with two sons at the altar and a prophet telling him so, while nearly every leader will one day hand a hard thing up the chain and go back to work. The handoff is rarely where it goes wrong. It goes wrong when the call is treated as the finish, and what was still in the leader's own hands to do is left undone.

In that last interview, Paterno put it this way. "I didn't know exactly how to handle it and I was afraid to do something that might jeopardize what the university procedure was. So I backed away and turned it over to some other people, people I thought would have a little more expertise than I did."

"I backed away." Those three words are where the two men meet. Eli rebuked his sons and Paterno made a phone call, and each of them stopped there.

**The mechanism**

Saying the problem out loud is where leading starts, and it is the cheap part, because words are vapor: they are gone from the room the moment they are said, and what stays behind is whatever the leader actually did. Action is real, and it is the only thing the people watching ever count. Josiah shows what that looks like. He was told the disaster was coming whatever he did, and he still went out and tore down the altars to other gods, including the ones his grandfather, King Manasseh, had built. Eli heard a word that named his own family, said "It is the LORD," and left his sons at the altar. Both men heard the hard truth and agreed with it. Only one of them moved.

Naming the problem feels like leading, and that is the trap. The leader has the conversation, says the hard thing out loud in the right words to the right person, and walks away with the relief of having said it without paying anything for it. That relief is why the same talk gets held again the next time the reports come in.

I wrote about the inside of this moment in [Toxic Empathy Post 2: Holding the Line](/journal/toxic-empathy-2-holding-the-line), where the leader knows exactly what needs to happen and keeps moving the conversation to next week. Eli shows where that ends when next week never comes. He kept his rebuke cheap for his sons, and the cost landed anyway: on worshipers shaken down at the altar, on thirty thousand soldiers, on a daughter-in-law dying in childbirth, and on a priesthood his family would never get back.

Eli's family was judged "for the iniquity that he knew," and what undid Paterno's legacy was also what he knew. A leader who names the problem and then leaves it as it was has still made a decision. He made it by staying in his seat.

Every leader has someone in a role right now who should not be holding it, and most of us have already had the talk. The question is what you are still getting out of keeping them there, and what news you will one day be sitting by the road waiting to hear.
