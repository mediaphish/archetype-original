# People First Leadership (McDonald County Leadership)

## The event

Bart Paden gave this talk on Wednesday, November 11, 2026, to the McDonald County Leadership class. People reached the talk page by scanning a QR code on the questions slide. The page is archetypeoriginal.com/mcdonald-county-leadership. It has the slides, Archy, and Bart's contact details.

## Runway

Bart got a C in his eighth grade programming class, and then he built a software company. He did not do it because he was gifted. At every step, somebody gave him something.

In 1993, at Missouri Southern, two professors introduced him to the internet. There was no YouTube and there were no books on building for the web. They let him figure it out, and he built a website for the School of Business.

In 1998 he interviewed for a marketing job at Precious Moments. He was asked to write a press release. He was an economics major and had no idea what a press release was, so he built a web page instead. They hired him anyway. Nobody planned for him to run the web, but they let him, and he grew a site making $20,000 a year into one making $1.2 million a year.

Those people did not give him a title or a plan. They gave him runway: room to try something, and the trust to run with it. In 2003 he started the company that became Midwestern Interactive, built it past 100 people, and sold it in 2022.

## The one idea

"If you grow people, you grow the organization. If you use people, you lose it." (Bart Paden)

Growing people looks like runway. Somebody gave it to Bart at every step. Leadership is your turn to give it to someone else.

## The best leader you ever had

The room was asked to think of the best leader they ever had, a coach, a teacher, a boss or a parent, and say one thing that person did. The answers usually sound like: they listened, they believed in me, they held me to it. None of those is about a title. Every one is a way of giving someone runway.

## Servant leadership

A servant puts someone else's needs first. Most people think leader and servant are opposites. Robert Greenleaf gave servant leadership its name in 1970 and said the servant leader is servant first, leader second.

Greenleaf also gave it a test. Not whether the work got done, but whether the people you serve grow. Do they become healthier, wiser, and more able to stand on their own? That is runway by another name.

## Not soft

Servant leadership can sound soft. Bart calls it the hardest way to lead. The people in your care matter more than the task, and two things follow: you put them ahead of yourself, and you hold the standard. Both, every time.

Putting them first: Bart's first hire was a young designer who was getting married and needed health insurance the company could not afford. Bart was a small partner, not the owner, and he gave up about $200 a month of his own income to cover it.

Holding the standard: when a project stalled because the person leading it made decisions for himself instead of the client, Bart gave the client back every dollar of the $30,000 they had paid. They lost the project and kept the relationship. The rule Bart took from it: when your team fails, you take the hit, but you do not take the lesson away from the person who caused it.

## Where Bart got it wrong

Bart once took on so much for the people around him that he ran himself empty, to the point of panic attacks in the middle of the night. Servant leadership is not martyrdom. It is stewardship, and that includes stewarding yourself: your sleep, your health, your faith, your margin. If you burn out trying to carry everyone, you are no good to anyone.

## How it ran day to day

At Midwestern Interactive the team was fed every week, anyone could join Bart for breakfast on Fridays, and 20 percent of every week was for growth instead of client work. It paid for itself, because people got better and stayed. The company also built MWI Cares, so anyone struggling had a real person to talk to. Many ended up on a leather couch in Bart's office that became known as the crying couch. His job was not to fix everything. It was to listen and help them take the next step.

## Three things for this week

Wherever you lead, at work, on a team, in a ministry or in your family:

1. **Give someone runway.** Hand one person something real to own, then stay out of their way but close enough to help.
2. **Hold one standard out loud.** Have the conversation you have been avoiding with someone you lead, kindly and clearly. That is taking them seriously.
3. **Guard your own tank.** Name one thing that keeps you full, like sleep, prayer or a day off, and protect it like it is part of the job.

## The banks

Bart thinks of his job as the banks of a river. He does not make the water move. He keeps it going where it should. "I'm not the river. I'm the banks."

## One question for the week

Who did I make better this week?
